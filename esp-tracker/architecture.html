Architecture v4 ESP32 · SIM800L · PH 28 Aug 2026

Four Fixes, One Uplink

A child tracker on 2G in the Philippines: four layered position sources, one bursty HTTPS uplink, and SMS as the channel that cannot be killed. The design turns on a single deadline — transmit at five seconds, whatever the fix looks like.

The constraint that shapes everything

GNSS does not work indoors, and indoors is where children spend their days. Satellite signal sits near −130 dBm and buildings attenuate it 10–30 dB; through a school's concrete you get nothing at all. A-GNSS does not rescue this — assistance data cuts time-to-first-fix, it does not improve sensitivity. So GNSS is demoted to one source among four, Wi-Fi scanning becomes the indoor primary, and the firmware is built around a transmit deadline rather than a fix quality target.

Revision note

Why Find My Device was cut

Removed in v3

v1 used Google's Find My Device network as the only channel; v2 kept it as a free fallback beneath a cellular primary. Both were wrong, and “add a free backup radio” is a tempting enough instinct to be worth recording why.

  1. Its coverage is a subset of what it was meant to back up. Detection needs nearby Android phones opted into the network — often several, since the setting commonly defaults to high-traffic areas only. Places dense with phone-carrying people are overwhelmingly places with cell coverage. It is weakest exactly where a fallback is needed.
  2. Its one real niche is already covered. Dense indoors with poor cellular is genuine FMDN territory — and it is precisely what the Wi-Fi scan does better, with no third party and no extra radio.
  3. It introduces a problem that otherwise does not exist. A device that advertises nothing trips no unwanted-tracker detection. Adding the beacon opts the child into “unknown tracker travelling with you” alerts on every nearby phone.

Also revised since: the SIM7080G was out of stock, so this builds on a SIM800L — 2G only, which is the right call in the Philippines where 2G is widely deployed and LTE-M effectively is not. That substitution cost us TLS on the modem and two days of battery, and bought us SMS as a channel that cannot be killed.

What we give up by dropping FMDN: a tracker that is off, dead, or out of coverage is simply gone. FMDN answers “where did the device go” — a lost-property question, not a child-safety one. Mitigated by last-known position with an honest age, a remote-triggered buzzer, and a short-range find mode the parent turns on only while searching.

Transport

Why HTTP beat MQTT here

The plan called for MQTT. Then the GPRS session had to become bursty rather than persistent — carrier NAT drops an idle TCP session after 1–5 minutes, and the ~60 s keepalives needed to fight that stop the modem ever reaching AT+CSCLK sleep, the biggest power lever available. You would spend the battery keeping alive a socket the carrier kills anyway.

Once the connection is bursty, MQTT's persistent session buys nothing, and plain HTTPS is simpler in three concrete ways: no broker to run or secure, so the ingest worker collapses into a FastAPI route; the 200 response is the ack, removing the trap where QoS 1 confirms delivery to the broker rather than to your server; and it is debuggable with curl, which on a 2G link you will want.

Two rules come with it. Batch, so the 3–8 s handshake is amortised over many points instead of paid per point. And give every event an id, so a retried POST is idempotent — a redelivery must never become a second position. One gotcha: TLS validation needs a roughly-correct clock and the ESP32 has no RTC, so pull the time from the modem with AT+CCLK? before the first handshake or every connection fails validation for no visible reason.

The uplink survives where the fix does not. LTE-M and especially NB-IoT were designed for basement utility meters — NB-IoT's coverage-enhancement modes buy roughly 20 dB of link budget over GSM. The SOS and the offline heartbeat keep working in places where a phone shows one bar. That asymmetry is the main argument for LTE-M over a plain LTE modem.

Known-place anchoring solves cost, latency and privacy at once — and the parent sets it up themselves, from the dashboard. See Figure 2.

Where it genuinely fails: underground parking, and rural interiors whose one access point is in no database. Both fall through to cell-tower ID — kilometres, not metres. There is no clean fix, so the honest response is to return source='cell' with a truthful radius and draw a two-kilometre circle. Never let the map imply precision the fix does not have.

Figure 2

The parent names the place

The schema always had a slot for “these networks are school.” What it never had was a way to fill it — and asking a parent to type in MAC addresses was never going to happen. Showing them DepEd-Classroom-3 and letting them tick it does.

BSSIDs are stored hashed; SSIDs are not. An access-point history is a finer-grained record of where a child has been than the coordinate history sitting next to it, so a database dump must not hand one over. But the parent needs something they can recognise to click on, and the matching runs on the hashes either way.

The five-second rule, indoors and out

The instinct is to wait for a good GNSS fix before transmitting. Outdoors that costs ten seconds. Indoors it costs everything, because the GNSS branch never returns at all.

Escalation, not fan-out

Sending every channel at once and hoping is not a design. The ladder assumes the first parent may be asleep, driving, or out of signal, and keeps climbing until a human acknowledges — at which point it stops dead and records who answered.

Power model

Report on state change, not on a timer

A fixed interval wastes the modem when the child has sat in a classroom for four hours, and is far too slow when they are in a car. Geofences are evaluated on-device, so a “left school” alert never waits for the next upload.

There is no accelerometer in the BOM, so motion is inferred from the radios already present: the serving cell every 60 s (free — the modem is awake anyway), and a Wi-Fi scan every 2–5 min compared against the last by Jaccard similarity. That scan is the authority, and it costs about 0.5% of the battery per day.

This is arguably the better signal. An accelerometer answers the wrong question — it reports that the device is being jostled, which a child fidgeting at a desk does all day, and every false “moving” burns a modem wake. A changed Wi-Fi neighbourhood reports that the child is somewhere else, which is what the cadence actually cares about. What is genuinely lost is interrupt-driven wake from sleep, which matters little when the modem outdraws the sleeping MCU by three orders of magnitude.

Target on a 2000 mAh cell is five days, charged every three or four. Measure with a PPK2 or INA219 — datasheet figures will not survive a real TLS handshake. Push a low-battery alert at 20% and 10%: a dead tracker still showing its last pin is the worst failure this system can produce.

Before Phase 00

Two things that still bite

Conflating position sources is the most dangerous bug available here. /api/devices must return source, accuracy_m and recorded_at alongside the coordinates, and the map must never render a two-kilometre cell estimate the way it renders a four-second GNSS fix. Age badge on every pin, ring sized by real accuracy, desaturate past 15 minutes — and where a known place matches, show the place instead of the pin.

Tell the child. Dropping the beacon removed the anti-stalking problem but not the human one. A tracker a child knows about is a safety tool; one they discover is surveillance, and the discovery destroys trust irreparably. Age-appropriate disclosure, let them see their own map, and get the other parent's agreement in writing — in shared-custody situations, unilateral tracking has been treated as harassment.
