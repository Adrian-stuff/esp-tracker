Hardware build reference

Tracker & Scanner Build

Two boards, wired and powered from the ground up: the wearable tracker a child carries, and the RFID scanner bolted at the school gate. Same rule breaks more builds than any bug does — get the power wiring right first.

Shared ground rules· The tracker· The scanner· How they talk to each other

Before wiring anything

Both boards carry a 2G modem, and both modems share the same trap: they pull far more current than they idle at, in short bursts, whenever they transmit. Everything below follows from that one fact.

The one rule that matters most
  • A GSM modem can pull up to 2 A for a fraction of a second while transmitting, at 3.4–4.4 V. Neither board's onboard 3.3 V regulator nor a USB port can supply that — give every modem its own direct feed at that voltage.
  • Put a 1000–2200 µF electrolytic capacitor across the modem's power pins, physically as close to the module as the wiring allows. Without it, the transmit burst dips the rail far enough to reset the whole board — which looks exactly like a firmware bug and wastes a weekend proving it isn't.
  • Use thick, short power wires to the modem. A thin jumper has enough resistance on its own to cause the same brownout.
  • The RFID reader and the GPS module are both 3.3 V only — 5 V destroys either one outright.

Bring a board up one part at a time — power the MCU alone first, then add one module, prove it works over serial, then add the next. Wiring everything at once and flashing the full firmware turns every fault into "nothing works," with no way to tell which part is actually broken. Both build orders below follow that same discipline.

B

How it's powered

The battery is the supply, not a backup. USB only ever reaches the TP4056, which charges the LiPo and nothing else. The LiPo's raw 3.7–4.2 V then goes straight to the SIM800L, because the modem's transmit bursts need far more current than the ESP32's onboard 3.3 V regulator can give. That same 3.3 V rail is fine for the ESP32 and the GPS module, which draw much less. Every ground is tied together across the whole board.

Build it in this order

1
ESP32 alone
Blink the onboard LED, blink the feedback LED, sound the piezo. Confirms the board, the supply, and the feedback wiring before anything else can confuse the picture.
2
GPS on its own
Power it from 3.3 V and watch its serial output. Expect GPS sentences within a minute outdoors, up to five indoors. Nothing coming through usually means TX/RX are swapped, or the module was run at 5 V and is dead.
3
Modem on its own supply
Power it from the LiPo, before any code runs. Confirm its status LED is blinking, then confirm it answers a basic command, shows it's registered on the network, and can send one text by hand.
4
SOS button
Confirm the pin reads low while held and high the rest of the time.
5
Full firmware
Only now. Everything gets wired together and flashed once every part has already proven itself alone.

B

How it's powered

The SIM900 has the same appetite as the tracker's modem — up to 2 A in bursts at 3.4–4.4 V — and the same rule applies: it can't run from the ESP32's 3.3 V pin or straight off USB 5 V. Give it its own regulator stepped down to roughly 4 V (or a shield that already has one), plus the same 1000–2200 µF bulk cap right at its power pins. Being on mains power removes the battery-budget question here, not the current spike.

The SIM900's power button isn't a power button

PWRKEY is a toggle, not an on switch — every ≈1.2 s LOW pulse flips the modem between on and off. That matters because the ESP32's own boot leaves GPIO 32 floating for a moment, and if it floats low, the modem's power state flips at random on every reset. Wire it exactly as speced, and let the firmware pin the line high the instant it starts — that's what makes the boot behavior predictable instead of a coin flip.

Get the LED wiring type right

If you use a single 4-pin RGB LED instead of two discrete ones, its wiring type has to match what the firmware expects. Common-cathode (the shared leg to ground, lit on HIGH) is the default assumption. Wire a common-anode LED without changing that setting and every cue inverts — on at idle, dark during an actual alert.

Why the clock isn't optional

The ESP32 has no clock of its own; it only knows the time by asking the network, and a school network will drop. The DS1302 keeps ticking through outages and reboots on its coin cell, so a tap that gets buffered during an outage still carries the moment it actually happened, not a guess made later when the connection comes back.

Modem's power state flips unpredictably across resetsExpected if PWRKEY isn't pinned high fast enough at boot — see the caution above

A card can also carry a backup contact directly on itself, for when the network truly can't be reached. That data sits behind a key that's deliberately not the factory default, so writing new cards means using the project's own card-writer tool with the matching key.

How they talk to each other

The two boards never share a wire. The tracker's location reports travel to the scanner purely as SMS, addressed to the scanner's SIM900 number; the scanner relays whatever it receives onward over its own Wi-Fi. An SOS from the tracker skips that whole path — it goes straight to the parent's phone by SMS, with no server and no scanner between them, so it survives either device being offline.

Pin maps are authoritative in tracker/include/pins.h and scanner/include/pins.h — if a pin ever changes, change it there first and this page second. Build: pio run -t upload -e tracker · pio run -t upload -e scanner
